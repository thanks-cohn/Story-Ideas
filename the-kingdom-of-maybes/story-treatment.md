# The Kingdom of Maybes

**Story treatment and character proposal**

**Genre:** Mythological dark fantasy, tragedy, rebellion, and ultimately a story about love.

**Working title and names:** I am using names so I can talk about everyone clearly. They are placeholders, not locked decisions. The central figures are the Son, the Father, Mother Gaia, the Devil, and the boy the Son grew up with. The relationships and story events matter more than any particular name.

## The people in the story

**Aren, the Son:** Aren is one of the Father's children, raised among his brothers and fellow soldiers in the kingdom. He looks like a young man, but he has lived within a divine order for far longer than an ordinary human life. He starts as a loyal son, a trained fighter, and someone who truly loves the Father. He is not born a rebel. What changes him is realizing that he and his brothers are loved only for as long as their lives fit the Father's plans. He has a patient, observant side that becomes important when he learns to use deception. He can grow into an impressive fighter without losing his ability to grieve. I want him to feel like someone who would cry over a stranger, even a stranger who would have killed him.

**The Father, also called the Lion King:** The Father is the ruling God of this world, with an imposing, lionlike face in his God-given form. I imagine him as both beautiful and frightening, with the dignity of a father and the appetite of a predator. He really does love his children, which makes the story worse. His love has never stopped him from killing or devouring them when his nature or his dream of a future kingdom demands it. He wants absolute love from every mind, body, and soul. He shows his subjects the people he has condemned to eternal suffering and expects praise for it. Even children around twelve, considered old enough to fight in his world, can be among the condemned. Anyone who refuses him risks being killed on the spot. He has built a religion in which affection, obedience, fear, and glory are almost impossible to separate.

**Gaia, the Mother:** Gaia is the maternal, living power of the world. Her connection to the Son is personal, not simply political. When he defects, she protects him from his Father's attempts to take him back. She offers him shelter, a place in the world beyond the kingdom, and access to older truths that the Father's official religion would prefer to bury. She is not there merely to tell Aren what to do. She loves him enough to defend his right to leave, even while she fears the cost of what he intends.

**Lucir, the Devil:** Lucir is this world's analogue of the Devil. He is an old adversary of the Father, clever, watchful, and much more interested in the uses of a mind than in brute strength. He teaches Aren deception, misdirection, and the use of semishadow figures or false presences that can confuse an enemy's senses and certainty. His relationship with Aren is not a simple pact where Aren trades one master for another. Lucir helps him learn a kind of power that his Father would not expect him to embrace. The Devil's smile at the end suggests that he recognizes what the Son has accomplished and perhaps what the two of them can do next.

**Toma, the childhood brother:** Toma is another son or adopted brother of the Father's household, someone Aren knew while growing up and training. The two share the habits and memories of boys raised to fight for the same Father. They are separated by Aren's defection and meet again much later, after Aren has suffered a disastrous defeat. Toma eventually defects too. His love for Aren becomes the emotional center of the later story. He knows they were taught whom to love, but he comes to believe that the love he feels for his brother is his own, not merely something placed in him by the kingdom. His final act costs him his life and makes Aren's final confrontation with the Father possible.

**The brothers and the dead:** These are not just background losses. Some are children of the Father who have already been killed or devoured by him, and others are soldiers and companions lost during the rebellion. Their absence should be felt long before they appear again as spirits. The story begins with their deaths being accepted, explained away, or quietly feared. It ends with their liberation.

**The Father's devotees:** The Father's armies include roughly three billion devoted followers, soldiers prepared to kill everyone who will not serve him. They sincerely believe that slaughter can bring their God glory, and that dying for him is an act of love. I do not want to treat them as faceless counters on a battlefield. They are people with families, memories, private affections, and names Aren will never know. Their planned violence is real, but so is the loss of their lives.

## The story

### Part One: The children of the Lion

I want the story to begin inside the Father's kingdom, before Aren ever thinks of rebellion. He and the other boys are brought up under a king they know as their Father. The Father's lion face is the face of God to them. It belongs in their prayers, their banners, their childhood memories, and the stories they are taught about why they exist.

The kingdom looks glorious from within, but there are absences everywhere. Brothers who once trained beside Aren are no longer there. Some have been killed or devoured by the Father himself. Everyone knows this, although people find ways to explain it away because they cannot imagine a life outside the Father's love. Others are promised a place in some distant, greater kingdom that he is always preparing to bring about.

The Father's subjects are shown glimpses of those he has damned. The suffering is not hidden as a shameful secret. It is displayed as proof of his authority. The people are expected to praise him for it, even when those condemned are young, or were once familiar to them. To disagree openly can mean immediate death. His rule does not ask only for outward obedience. It asks that people love him utterly while looking at what he has done.

Aren has spent a long time trying to hold two things together: the Father he loves and the Father who keeps killing those who love him. Eventually he confronts him. He points out that everyone knows what has happened to their brothers and that, no matter how much the children love their Father or stand by him, he will eventually come for each of them too. He cannot understand why the Father needs the lives of the people already here to pursue a faraway kingdom made of maybes.

The confrontation breaks him. He falls to his knees and asks whether he, too, will be killed in time. The Father, looking down at him with his lion face, acknowledges that this is his nature. Aren looks up, hoping for something more, and finds that the Father has already gone. He is left kneeling there with the answer.

That is the beginning of his break from the kingdom. He does not suddenly stop loving his Father. He realizes that his love will never make him safe, and that his brothers' love never saved them either.

### Part Two: Leaving the kingdom

Aren defects. The Father does not accept that his son can simply leave, and sends people to reclaim him. Aren is pursued by the world that raised him, including soldiers who remember him as a brother and now regard him as a traitor.

Mother Gaia intervenes. She shelters Aren and defends him against his Father's attempts to bring him back. In her care, he begins to see a world that does not belong entirely to the Father's commands. There are homes, fields, ordinary human lives, and bonds that exist without someone demanding worship in return. I want this stretch of the story to be quieter at moments. It is important that Aren has something real to defend, not only a God to defeat.

He also learns that the Father's reign is much larger than the losses he saw while growing up. His Father has killed and condemned people on an immense scale. Those he sends to Hell are not simply dead. They remain there to suffer without end as long as his order continues. More will follow, potentially trillions, if his armies carry out the purges he has planned.

Aren gathers men who are willing to resist. Some are defectors and some are people who would otherwise be victims of the Father's expansion. He knows the kingdom's fighting methods from the inside, but knowing them does not make the rebellion easy. The Father is a God, and his armies have numbers, training, and certainty on their side.

The first campaigns teach Aren how much it costs to defend people. He cannot protect everyone. He sees soldiers die under his command, and the old fear from his childhood returns in a new form. He has left the Father, yet men are still dying around him because of the path he has chosen.

### Part Three: The Devil's lesson

Around the middle of the story, Aren meets Lucir. The encounter should feel unsettling because Aren has been raised to think of the Devil as the enemy of everything sacred. Lucir does not win him over with a speech about goodness. He shows him that the Father can be misled.

Aren learns to work with semishadow forms, false positions, misleading appearances, and the difference between what an opponent sees and what is actually happening. This is not just a visual trick. The Father has always relied on his own overwhelming power and on his belief that he understands the nature of everyone beneath him. Lucir teaches Aren how to take advantage of certainty.

Aren remains suspicious of his teacher, and I want that relationship to retain some warmth and a little danger. The Devil enjoys seeing the Father's assumptions challenged, but Aren's reason for fighting is his own. He wants the condemned freed and the living spared. He is not leaving one Father's kingdom just to become someone else's servant.

The lessons give Aren a new way to fight. He becomes less predictable and more patient. His battle tactics begin to involve feints, shadowlike doubles, and setting up an enemy to believe that one event is happening while another is taking place.

Even with these new skills, he suffers another terrible defeat. The battle costs him about half his men. He retreats with those he can save. The experience should not be brushed aside as a simple lesson in strategy. Aren knows that every lost soldier had a life that will not continue in the form it should have.

### Part Four: The brother he finds again

During the retreat and its aftermath, Aren encounters Toma, someone he grew up with in the Father's household. Toma has also reached the point where he can no longer serve the Father. He defects and joins Aren.

Their reunion gives the story a different kind of closeness. They remember what it was like to be boys together, before the losses became impossible to ignore. They can remember ordinary, almost silly things from training, the people they both knew, and what they believed their lives would be. Toma is one of the few people who understands exactly what Aren has lost by leaving.

They come to speak about the love they were taught to feel for their Father and each other. The kingdom trained them to regard their bonds as duties of devotion, but Toma believes his love for Aren has become something independent of that training. He loves his brother because he loves him. He wants Aren to live well, not because a God has commanded it, but because his brother's life matters to him.

Toma is not just there to die. I want the two of them to spend enough time together that we feel the possibility of a future in which they might have grown older, argued, laughed, and built a life outside the Father's reach.

As the rebellion approaches its last campaign, Aren and Toma know the Father cannot be defeated by strength alone. Aren has learned how to make a divine opponent vulnerable, but he still needs a way to keep the Father from restoring his power and returning to torment the world.

### Part Five: The old rites

Through Gaia's knowledge of the older world, Lucir's understanding of deception, and Aren's familiarity with the Father's worship, an ancient possibility comes into view. The Father cannot simply be killed and forgotten. The old rites can bind him, and a far greater rite can send him into Sheol, the realm from which he cannot continue ruling the living or holding the condemned.

The cost is almost beyond imagining. Keeping the Father bound would require the sacrifice of an enormous part of the world, perhaps half of it. Sending him into Sheol requires a different, vast offering gathered through an older ritual system that the Father no longer expects anyone to use against him.

Aren comes to understand that the Father's three billion devotees can complete that rite. They are already preparing to slaughter everyone who refuses their God. They have been taught to regard death in his service as glorious. Through deception, Aren can redirect their devotion into the older ceremony. They will believe that they are helping bring about the Father's triumph, and some will be glad to give their lives for the being they believe is addressing them.

But the real effect of the rite will be to sacrifice them and send their Father into Sheol.

This is the decision that disgusts Aren. He sees the trap in its entirety and knows exactly what it makes him capable of doing. He rebelled because the Father sacrificed children and subjects for an imagined future. Now he must choose whether to sacrifice an army that loves the Father in order to prevent the eternal suffering of trillions.

There is an important difference in the fate of the dead. The people the Father kills are condemned to rot in Hell forever. The devotees Aren sacrifices will instead become spirits or be annihilated, depending on what the rite does to each of them. Their deaths are terrible, but he is not consigning them to the Father's endless torture. The sacrificed army does not become another population of prisoners in Hell.

He still does not like the choice. He does not make peace with the deaths by calling them glorious. He understands the scale of what he is about to do, prepares the deception, and carries the decision himself.

### Part Six: The last battle

The final campaign brings Aren face to face with the Father again. By now he has become a formidable fighter, one who can use the Father's own expectations against him. The semishadow techniques and misdirection that once seemed like tricks now determine the battle.

Toma stays beside him through the assault. At a decisive moment, Toma makes a fatal choice of his own to keep Aren alive and give him the opening he needs. Aren does not ask him to do it and does not want him to die. Toma's final assurance is that his love for his brother was never merely a command planted by their upbringing. He wants Aren to live well, and he has always loved him.

The loss is almost unbearable. Aren must keep going with the knowledge that this brother, too, is gone, and that he cannot undo what happened.

He returns to the fight against the Father. The Father still believes the son he raised cannot truly threaten him. Aren lets him believe he has the advantage, then deceives him and lands a wound.

I imagine the Father's reaction happening in stages. His face changes. He looks pale. His eyes go wide. He looks down at his hand and sees the injury, then recognizes the slash that should not have been possible. The powerful Lion King, who has stood over his children all their lives, turns and runs. Aren chases him.

The chase brings them to the ancient ritual ground, where the Father's devotees have been carrying out what they believe is an act of supreme worship. Their ceremony is already in motion. Its scale has seemed like another sign that the Father's glory is about to be fulfilled.

Then the Father recognizes the geometry of the old rite. He understands that he has entered the place where he can be bound and sent below. He understands the terrible amount of life such a rite requires, and he assumes his son could never have paid it.

Aren reveals what has actually happened. The Father's own army supplied the sacrifice. They were prepared to destroy the people who refused him, yet they have been drawn into a ceremony that consumes them instead. They believed they were dying for their Father's victory. They did not know that their devotion was being turned against him.

This is the act the Father never anticipated from his son. He knew Aren hated him for sacrificing people who loved him. He assumed that hatred meant Aren would never do the same kind of thing, even to stop him. Aren has done it, with full knowledge of what it costs.

The Father realizes that the love and obedience he cultivated in his followers have made his downfall possible. The ritual takes hold. He can no longer simply command the world to free him. He is sent into Sheol.

The scene can draw on the frightening, ancient imagery of divine judgment and descent into Hell, but I want the ending of this confrontation to stay personal. Aren is not gloating over a stranger. He is watching the Father he once loved disappear. He has finally understood that his Father killed his beloved children for people who might exist someday, for a kingdom always out of reach. He sends him away to that land of make-believe, then plants his sword in the ground.

### Part Seven: What happens to the dead

Only after the Father's fall do we fully see what his defeat has changed. The souls he killed, devoured, or condemned are released from his dominion. The brothers Aren lost are among them. So are those who suffered in Hell, including people who had no way to resist him while alive.

The Father, meanwhile, is made to endure the suffering he intended for others. His punishment corresponds to the immense eternity of torment that he planned and imposed. He is no longer standing above the condemned and demanding praise from those forced to watch.

There is no need to make this a scene of uncomplicated celebration. Aren has freed the imprisoned and prevented the planned slaughter of trillions, but he has also caused the deaths of the Father's three billion devotees.

I want a moment after the victory where Aren grieves over soldiers he never met. He knows they would have killed him and the people he was trying to save. He knows the ritual prevented something vastly worse. Yet he is devastated that these individual people are gone. He never learned their names. He never knew their families, their childhoods, or who they might have been outside the Father's army. He cries because they existed, and now they do not live as they once did.

He does not need anyone to praise the sacrifice or pretend it was beautiful. His grief remains even though he believes he had to act.

### Part Eight: The little house

After the great religious and cosmic struggle, I want the story to end somewhere very small and peaceful. There is a little home in the Japanese countryside, with a quiet rural landscape around it. The scale of the final image should feel almost surprising after the throne rooms, battles, ritual ground, and descent into Sheol.

Aren is there with the spirits of the people he lost. His brothers and comrades can speak with him again, not as prisoners of the Father, but as themselves. Toma is among the beloved dead whose presence he can finally meet without the Father's shadow over them.

Aren smiles. This does not mean that every loss has been repaired or that he has forgotten the three billion devotees. He can be happy to see his brothers and still mourn the strangers whose deaths made this possible.

Lucir is there as well, smiling at what has happened and perhaps at what is still to come. Aren says goodbye to his comrades for now, tells them there is work to do, and runs off smiling. The house stays behind in the peaceful countryside.

That is the final image before the credits. The Father wanted an endless kingdom that justified every sacrifice. The Son ends up with something modest, living relationships, the freedom of the dead, and a future he can choose for himself.

## What I want the story to mean

I want the progression to begin with a son who genuinely loves his Father. Otherwise the betrayal is too easy. The first wound is not a battle wound. It is the moment the Father calmly admits that he will someday kill his son because that is his nature, then leaves him kneeling alone.

The lion imagery is important because the Father should feel majestic, protective, and predatory at once. He is capable of affection, but affection will never make him stop consuming his children. His kingdom teaches people to call that love and to praise him even while he shows them the eternal suffering of those who resisted him, including children.

Gaia gives Aren a way to leave without becoming rootless. Lucir gives him a way to fight without merely matching the Father's strength. Toma gives him a love that is freely felt rather than demanded. Together those relationships let the Son become someone other than a smaller version of the Father.

The final sacrifice is supposed to hurt. It is three billion against trillions, but that is not just a comparison of numbers. The Father's victims would suffer eternally, and the Father's planned purges would condemn still more. Aren's sacrificed devotees die and become spirits or are annihilated. They are not sentenced to eternal torture. Aren chooses the only path he can see that frees the imprisoned and stops the Father from doing this again. Even then, he has deceived people into dying, and he never pretends their deaths do not matter.

The Father never anticipates that choice because he thinks the Son's horror at sacrifice means he will always refuse to make one. Aren is willing to do the terrible thing he hates to put an end to the Father's much greater and unending cruelty. The fact that he later cries for the devotees, including people he never knew, is not an afterthought. It shows what the Father lacks. The Father shows his subjects the suffering he caused and demands praise. Aren remembers the people he killed and mourns them without asking anyone to celebrate him.

**The intended theological contrast:** Although I use Father and Son imagery, I want Aren to be an antithesis of Jesus in the way this story understands that figure. I am imagining a son who will not defend, adore, or excuse the Father for the horrors he commits. The point is not that this savior deserves reverence simply because he is divine, or because the indignity and mechanical suffering of a deity in human flesh are treated as automatically meaningful. He is worthy of love and, in the story's provocative sense, worship because he truly loves humanity and wants people freed from his Father's evil. He opposes his Father, bears the cost of that opposition, and grieves even for enemies he has had to sacrifice. The salvation here is liberation from the Father's rule, not obedience to it.

And after all that, I still want the ending to be warm. Not another throne. Not another demand for glory. Just a little house, the spirits of people he loves, the Devil smiling, and a boy running off toward the work he has chosen.
